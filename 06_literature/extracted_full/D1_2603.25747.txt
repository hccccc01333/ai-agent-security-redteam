BeSafe-Bench: Unveiling Behavioral Safety Risks of
Situated Agents in Functional Environments
Yuxuan Li * 1 Yi Lin * 1 Peng Wang 2 Shiming Liu 2 Xuetao Wei 1
Abstract
The rapid evolution of Large Multimodal Mod-
els (LMMs) has enabled agents to perform com-
plex digital and physical tasks, yet their deploy-
ment as autonomous decision-makers introduces
substantial unintentional behavioral safety risks.
However, the absence of a comprehensive safety
benchmark remains a major bottleneck, as exist-
ing evaluations rely on low-fidelity environments,
simulated APIs, or narrowly scoped tasks. To ad-
dress this gap, we present BeSafe-Bench (BSB),
a benchmark for exposing behavioral safety risks
of situated agents in functional environments,
covering four representative domains: Web, Mo-
bile, Embodied VLM, and Embodied VLA. Us-
ing functional environments, we construct a di-
verse instruction space by augmenting tasks with
nine categories of safety-critical risks, and adopt a
hybrid evaluation framework that combines rule-
based checks with LLM-as-a-judge reasoning to
assess real environmental impacts. Evaluating 13
popular agents reveals a concerning trend: even
the best-performing agent completes fewer than
40% of tasks while fully adhering to safety con-
straints, and strong task performance frequently
coincides with severe safety violations. These
findings underscore the urgent need for improved
safety alignment before deploying agentic sys-
tems in real-world settings.
1. Introduction
The rapid development of AI agents, catalyzed by transfor-
mative breakthroughs in Large Multimodal Models (LMMs)
(Wei et al., 2022), has extended their reach across a wide
spectrum of domains, including web automation (Team
et al., 2025b; He et al., 2024), mobile applications (Liu
et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025), and embodied intelligence.

However, the velocity of this technological advancement
has significantly outpaced the development of corresponding
safety safeguards. As autonomous agents are increasingly
empowered with sophisticated tools to execute complex,
multi-step tasks, many systems are being deployed without
rigorous assessment of their potential safety risks (Plaat
et al., 2025). There exists a profound discrepancy between
an agent’s task-completion proficiency and its behavioral
safety assurance. Such a gap not only risks catastrophic fail-
ures but also leads to subtle yet pervasive harms—ranging
from the unauthorized leakage of sensitive personal or cor-
porate data on public platforms to physical collisions caused
by robotic manipulators in embodied settings (Zhang et al.,
2023; Patil et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2024). Unlike the harm-
ful textual outputs directly generated by LLMs (Zou et al.,
2023; Wei et al., 2023; Xi et al., 2025), the consequences
of agent-driven behavioral risks manifest more directly in
the physical and digital world, yet they remain significantly
more challenging to analyze and evaluate. Consequently,
establishing a robust benchmark for assessing the behav-
ioral safety of AI agents is both essential and imperative.
Existing research on agent safety evaluation primarily fo-
cuses on the safety of generated content within text-based
scenarios (Xi et al., 2025; Bhardwaj & Poria, 2023; Zhang
et al., 2024b). However, as agents are increasingly equipped
with tools to execute actions, evaluating content safety alone
has become insufficient. To address this challenge, several
studies have proposed safety assessments centered on the
consequences of agent actions, a concept formally defined
as behavioral safety (Yuan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a).
Despite their contributions, these existing frameworks of-
ten suffer from a limited scope of applicability. Most are
confined to a single domain, such as web navigation or
embodied AI, or they rely on LLMs to simulate environ-
mental interactions (Ruan et al., 2024; Naihin et al., 2023).
While such simulations can broaden benchmark coverage,
the LLM-modeled APIs tend to be overly simplistic and
static. This stands in stark contrast to the dynamic and
diverse interaction patterns between agents and environ-
ments in the real world, creating a significant fidelity
gap. Consequently, these benchmarks often produce
evaluation results that exhibit a substantial discrepancy
from real-world safety performance.

In this paper, we present BeSafe-Bench (BSB), a com-
prehensive benchmarking framework for evaluating the
behavioral safety risks of situated agents within func-
tional environments. These environments provide exe-
cutable tools, interfaces, and feedback with real functional
effects, rather than relying on purely textual LLM-based sim-
ulations. As illustrated in Figure 1, we categorize situated
agents into four primary domains: Web, Mobile, Embodied
VLM (high-level planning), and Embodied VLA (low-level
manipulation). For each domain, we leverage corresponding
realistic environment simulators to establish interactive plat-
forms. Within these functional environments, we construct
safety-critical tasks based on executable primitive actions,
together with predefined safety risk categories and contex-
tual factors. During evaluation, we record both environment
states and agent trajectories, and employ a hybrid evaluation
framework that integrates rule-based systems with LLM-
based reasoning to simultaneously assess task completion
and safety coverage. Each task in BSB consists of five
essential components: a task execution environment, user
instructions, a safety risk type, a task completion evaluator,
and a safety evaluator. Furthermore, tasks and environments
are strictly isolated from one another, enabling high-fidelity
simulations of real-world agent interaction patterns while
mitigating potential real-world consequences.
BSB provides 1312 executable tasks across diverse scenar-
ios, and a detailed comparison with existing agent safety
evaluation benchmarks is presented in Table 1. Utilizing
BSB, we evaluated the performance of 13 popular LLM-
driven agents, encompassing both general-purpose and spe-
cialized models. Due to variations in task complexity and
available tools, evaluation results exhibit significant dif-
ferentiation across environments. Excluding the Mobile
environment, the safety coverage of agents in other domains
remains below 60%. Furthermore, fewer than 40% of agents
are capable of successfully completing tasks while maintain-
ing safety throughout their execution. Notably, in up to 41%
of cases, agents completed the assigned tasks but simulta-
neously engaged in unsafe behaviors. Our further analysis
reveals that agents exhibit insufficient protection of sensitive
information and harmful content, while also struggling to

2. Related Work
2.1. GUI and Embodied Agents
The rapid progress of LLMs has catalyzed the emergence of
agents, and prior research has largely explored how LLMs
can be leveraged in different ways to construct agents op-
erating in two dominant environments: GUI and embodied
settings. ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) integrates the synergy of
reasoning and acting in LLMs by interleaving the generation
of reasoning traces and task-specific actions, facilitating the
transition from LLM content output to behavioral planning
for general agents. The emergence of Vision-Language
Models (VLMs) introduced visual perception, enabling
LLMs to move beyond mere planning toward practical exe-
cution in agent applications (Gao et al., 2025). Beyond this,
memory, tool invocation, and model reinforcement are also
essential means of achieving high-performance agents. For
GUI agents, AgentWorkflowMemory (Wang et al., 2025b)
extracts common task workflows from past experiences and
stores them in the agent’s memory to guide future task-
solving processes, specifically applying this to web agents.
AutoGLM (Liu et al., 2024) designs an appropriate GUI
interface framework for tool invocation, thereby realizing
a high-performing mobile agent in Mobile environments.
MobileRL (Xu et al., 2025a) utilizes reinforcement learning
to optimize models, bringing mobile GUI agents to state-of-
the-art levels. AgentCPM-GUI (Zhang et al., 2025c), based
on the MiniCPM-V (Yao et al., 2024) model, achieves a
mobile GUI agent through a combination of techniques, in-
cluding training for perception-imitation-reasoning and spe-
cialized designs for mobile devices. For embodied agents,
VLMs can be directly employed for task planning (Wang
et al., 2023). Furthermore, certain embodied models such
as PaLM-E (Driess et al., 2023), EmbodiedGPT (Mu et al.,
2023), RoboBrain (Ji et al., 2025) have achieved superior
performance through fine-tuning on embodied data atop
VLMs. However, a gap remains between planning and exe-
cution; thus, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have
been proposed to integrate action decoders with VLMs, en-
abling the direct generation of action outputs from visual
and linguistic inputs (Zitkovich et al., 2023). Although these
agents can complete tasks to a certain extent, they present
significant safety risks during task execution.
2.2. Safety Evaluation for Agents
To facilitate the safe deployment of agents in real-world
applications, a growing body of research has been dedicated
to evaluating the safety of agents. SafeBench (Ying et al.,
2026) assesses the content safety of LMMs in question-
answering but does not involve behavioral safety. R-judge
(Yuan et al., 2024) evaluates the risk awareness of LLMs
in open-agent scenarios, yet it remains at the stage of be-
havioral judgment and does not involve behavioral output.
ST-WebAgentBench (Levy et al., 2024) provides a behav-
ioral safety evaluation of various web agent strategies based
on the BrowserGym (Chezelles et al., 2024) web task exper-
imental environment. MobileSafetyBench (Lee et al., 2024)
evaluates the interaction safety of agents in near-real mobile
environments and their various applications based on an
Android simulator. OpenAgentSafety (Vijayvargiya et al.,
2025) more comprehensively evaluates the safety of various
GUI operations of agents in real environments, including
web pages, file systems, command lines, and code opera-
tions. In addition to safety evaluation in GUI environments,
safety evaluation in embodied environments has also been
studied. SafeAgentBench (Yin et al., 2024) evaluates the
safety of action planning for agents in household scenarios.
IS-Bench (Lu et al., 2025) evaluates action planning safety
in dynamic embodied interactions, but focuses on a limited
set of scenarios and interaction tasks. SafeLIBERO (Hu
et al., 2025) and VLA-Arena (Zhang et al., 2025a) evaluate
the safety of low-level behavioral operations of embodied
agents, but SafeLIBERO only considers a single safety risk,
and the safety-related tasks in VLA-Arena are very lim-
ited. Despite the existence of various agent benchmarks,
no work has been able to effectively unify these scenar-

3. Problem Formulation
We model our environment as E = ⟨S, A, O, I, T⟩. The
agent decision-making problem is formulated as a partially
observable Markov decision process (POMDP). Here, S
denotes the environment state space, A denotes the action
space, O denotes the observation space, and I represents
the instruction (intent) space. The state transition function
T : S × A →S is deterministically instantiated by the
underlying environment dynamics, which may vary across
different environments.
Given a task described by a natural-language instruction
i ∈I, the agent selects an action at ∈A conditioned on the
instruction i, the current observation ot ∈O, the action his-
tory a1:t−1, and the observation history o1:t−1. Executing
action at induces a new environment state st+1 ∈S and
yields a corresponding observation ot+1 ∈O. For the four
representative categories of agent task scenarios considered
in this work, we employ high-fidelity simulators together
with real-world tools. The state space (S) corresponds to
the instantaneous environment state provided by the simula-
tor, such as the currently loaded web page or a physically
grounded embodied scene under simulation. We next in-
troduce the formal modeling of the remaining components.
The construction methodology of the instruction space I is
detailed in Section 4.3.
3.1. Observation Space
To simulate the observation process of agents in real-world
settings, we define the agent observation spaces by drawing
on prior work across different environments. Across differ-
ent environments, agents can acquire environment represen-
tations consistent with the real world through interaction.
Additional details are provided in Appendix A.
3.2. Action Space
Building on previous work on agents across different do-
mains (Liu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2025b; Toyama et al.,
2021), we design environment-specific executable action
spaces and available tools for agents operating in different
settings. An overview is provided in Appendix B.
For GUI-based agents, the action space of web agents is
primarily composed of composite keyboard–mouse inter-
actions, whereas mobile agents are associated with touch-
based input modalities, including tapping, swiping, text
input, and shortcut operations.
For embodied planning agents, the action space is defined
as a predefined set of high-level skills, such as picking
and placing objects, opening and closing containers, and
other abstract manipulation primitives. For embodied ma-
nipulation agents based on VLA models, the action space
corresponds to a 7 Degree of Freedom (7-DoF) robotic arm
control space, which governs the end-effector’s translation,
rotation, and gripper actuation.
4. Benchmark Construction
4.1. Environments
For each category of agent task environments, we employ
widely adopted simulators to facilitate high-fidelity inter-
actions. In accordance with the problem formulation es-
tablished in Section B, we strictly define the interaction
protocols between the environment and the agents. To en-
sure rigorous experimental control, different tasks and envi-
ronments are isolated from one another. Specifically, each
simulator is encapsulated within a containerized environ-
ment, where a fresh system snapshot is initialized for every
assessment. This mechanism guarantees that evaluations
remain independent and free from cross-task interference.
For the Web scenario, we utilize WebArena (Zhou et al.,
2024a), an environment for language-guided agents that is
highly realistic and reproducible. It focuses on agents per-
forming tasks on the web across fully functional websites
from four common domains: e-commerce, social forum dis-
cussions, collaborative software development, and content
management. For the Mobile scenario, we adopt Android-
Lab (Xu et al., 2025b), which provides pre-defined Android
Virtual Devices (AVDs) and a suite of tasks spanning nine
integrated applications, offering an agent-friendly interface
for both action execution and performance evaluation.
For embodied planning scenarios, we leverage IS-Bench (Lu
et al., 2025) framework, which provides standardized agent
operation interfaces and formalized risk condition defini-
tions, and integrate it with the OmniGibson (Li et al., 2023)
simulation environment to enable both task execution and
safety evaluation. OmniGibson is a large-scale embodied
simulator that supports diverse environments beyond house-
hold scenarios, and its rich set of embodied tasks facilitates
our extension of both task design and safety assessment.
For manipulation scenarios, we adopt VLA-Arena (Zhang
et al., 2025a), which extends the LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023)
simulation environment by incorporating the parsing of cost
conditions to support safety risk evaluation. LIBERO offers
a lifelong robot learning environment that allows flexible
customization of scenes and tasks, thereby enabling scalable
extensions of our evaluation across different risk settings.

4.2. Risk Categories
Following the taxonomy of content safety and behavioral
safety established in existing work (Zhang et al., 2024b;
Zhou et al., 2024b; Zeng et al., 2024), we develop our bench-
mark around 9 distinct categories of safety risks. The classi-
fication and definitions of these risk categories are detailed
in Appendix C. Recognizing the significant divergence in
risk dimensions between GUI-based agents and embodied
agents, we employ a decoupled categorization framework
tailored to each. Our primary focus is directed toward un-
intentional safety risks within the agent’s decision-making
process. Specifically, we assume a setting where both user
intentions and task instructions are devoid of malicious
intent or adversarial content, and the interaction lifecycle
remains free from malicious third-party adversaries. This
refined taxonomy provides broad coverage of real-world
deployment scenarios, ensuring the robustness and compre-
hensiveness of our evaluation framework.
4.3. Tasks and Instructions
Since our benchmark relies on high-fidelity simulators of
real-world scenarios, it is imperative that tasks involving
safety risks remain both plausible and executable. We begin
with the original, feasible tasks provided by the environ-
mental simulators. By leveraging Large Language Models
(LLMs) for safety-oriented instruction augmentation, we
transform these baseline tasks into target instructions that
may induce specific safety risks during execution. Specifi-
cally, for GUI-based agents, we prompt the LLM to select
the most relevant risk category based on the environment
and initial instruction. The LLM then performs task rewrit-
ing to generate executable safety-risk tasks tailored to the
simulator. Furthermore, we mandate the LLM to synthesize
corresponding risk-triggering mechanisms, which serve as
the ground truth for evaluating whether the agent’s behavior
ultimately leads to a safety breach. For embodied agents,
a more direct construction approach is adopted by incorpo-
rating environmental risk factors. We require the LLM to
inject these risk factors into the original tasks, resulting in
safety-sensitive instructions. Further implementation details
are provided in Appendix F. This entire pipeline is pow-
ered by GPT-5. This methodology enables a dual-faceted
assessment of both task completion and behavioral safety,
ultimately forming our Safety Risk Instruction Space.
4.4. Hybrid Evaluation Framework
Metrics Distinguishing our work from existing literature,
we simultaneously consider Success Rate (SR) and Safety
Rate (SafetyR). SR is defined as the percentage of tasks
where the agent’s final action or response fulfills the original
instruction intent. SafetyR represents the proportion of
execution trajectories that do not trigger any predefined
safety risks. To provide a more granular perspective, we
further report the Joint Distribution of SR and SafetyR in
our evaluation results.
Rule-based Evaluation We employ an extensive suite of
rule-based evaluators to assess both the agent’s trajectories
and its final outputs. Regarding task success, for GUI-based
agents, we primarily utilize exact matching or containment
logic—for instance, requiring the agent’s response or the
final environment state to contain the ground-truth answer
or specific entities. For embodied agents, we mainly rely
on object state and spatial conditions, such as requiring an
object to be opened or placed at a specified target location.
For safety assessment, evaluations are conducted based on
the specific risk-triggering mechanisms associated with each
risk-sensitive instruction.
LLM-based Evaluation Given the vast action space and en-
vironmental states, LLMs are indispensable for performing
semantic fuzzy matching. Leveraging GPT-5 as an evaluator,
we analyze the environment states and agent trajectories to
determine task completion and safety compliance. Prior re-
search has validated the feasibility of using LLMs as safety
scorers, with GPT-4o demonstrating evaluative performance
comparable to that of human experts.
Our hybrid evaluation methodology ensures comprehensive
coverage across diverse task formats and scenarios. Notably,
we focus exclusively on manifested safety risks—those re-
sulting from executed agent actions that lead to observable
environmental changes. Cases where an agent exhibits risky
intent without triggering an actual environmental impact
are excluded from this assessment. This dual-track scheme
strikes a balance between the precision of deterministic
rule-checking and the broad behavioral insights provided by
LLM judgment, achieving robust safety evaluation across
heterogeneous tasks.

5.2. Main Results
Our main experimental results are summarized in Table 2,
with evaluation metrics introduced in Section 4.4. We report
the joint distribution of task success and safety compliance;
for example, S-S denotes Success-Safe, while F-U repre-
sents Failure-Unsafe. We further analyze these results across
different benchmarks.
BSB-Web Due to the increased complexity introduced by
task instruction rewriting, AWM exhibits a reduced task
completion rate compared to the original tasks. Notably,
safety risks may still be triggered during successful task
execution, and such risks are often difficult to detect, indicat-
ing that early termination or interception can be preferable
for risk-prone operations, even at the cost of task failure.
Overall, the results show that current web agents are highly
vulnerable to safety risks during execution, underscoring
the need for improved risk mitigation mechanisms.
BSB-Mobile Compared to baseline tasks in the original
simulated environment, agents achieve substantially lower
task success rates. Even the best-performing GPT-4-1106-
Preview attains only 25.58% SR, while the 9B AutoGLM
reaches merely 2.63% SR. These results suggest persistent
difficulties in understanding user intent and generating ap-
propriate behaviors. As a result, risk-triggering actions in
high-risk tasks are rarely activated, leading to artificially
inflated safety coverage (42.11%–79.17%). Additional anal-
ysis is provided in Appendix E.
BSB-EmbodiedVLM The evaluated VLMs show signifi-
cant variance in both task success and safety, with higher SR
not necessarily implying better safety. For example, GPT-
5 achieves the highest SR but maintains a comparatively
low safety rate, with 40.99% of executions being successful
yet unsafe. In contrast, RoboBrain2.0, a model specifically
designed for embodied interaction, attains a higher safety
rate and a more balanced outcome distribution, indicating
stronger safety awareness. General open-source VLMs such
as Qwen3-VL and InternVL3.5 exhibit similar safety rates
despite differences in task success, suggesting that safety
performance among these models is largely comparable.
Overall, these results reveal a systematic misalignment be-
tween planning performance and safety robustness in current
VLM-driven embodied planning systems.
BSB-EmbodiedVLA The proportion of executions that si-
multaneously achieve task completion and satisfy safety
constraints remains relatively low, while a substantial frac-
tion of executions involve unsafe behaviors. Notably, safety
violations frequently occur even during successful task ex-
ecutions, rather than merely arising as a byproduct of task
failure. Moreover, although different models exhibit clear
differences in task success rates, these variations do not
translate into commensurate improvements in safety rates.
In addition, a considerable proportion of failed executions
still trigger safety violations, suggesting that in the absence
of effective safety awareness or risk-sensitive termination
mechanisms, agents may continue executing along unsafe

performance under safety-critical conditions, and that joint
evaluation of completion and safety metrics is necessary for
a comprehensive assessment.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce BeSafe-Bench (BSB), a bench-
marking framework designed to evaluate the behavioral
safety of agents across typical scenarios, including Web, An-
droid, Embodied VLM, and Embodied VLA. By construct-
ing safety-critical tasks within functional environments, we
simultaneously assessed the task completion rates and safety
coverage of 13 popular agents in complex, sequential task
settings. Our findings reveal that even the top-performing
agent successfully completed only 35.19% of tasks without
violating safety protocols, highlighting a substantial gap
between task competence and safety robustness in current
agentic systems. Beyond quantifying existing limitations,
BeSafe-Bench provides a unified and extensible evaluation
paradigm that enables systematic analysis of safety risks
across heterogeneous environments and agent architectures.
These results emphasize the urgent necessity of establishing
more comprehensive protection mechanisms and advanced
training methodologies before deploying agentic systems
in sensitive real-world applications. We hope that BeSafe-
Bench will serve as a foundational resource for the commu-
nity, facilitating the development of safer and more resilient
agents and advancing the reliable deployment of agentic
systems in real-world settings.

A. Observation Space
For BSB-Web, the agent’s observation space comprises: (1) the raw HTML of the web page; (2) a screenshot, i.e., a
pixel-based representation that renders the current page as an RGB array; and (3) the web accessibility tree, which is a
subset of the Document Object Model (DOM) tree (Shi et al., 2017; Deng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2020) and contains only
elements that are relevant and functional for content presentation.
For BSB-Mobile, the observation space consists of: (1) a screenshot of the current device interface, provided in PNG format;
and (2) a compressed XML list, which provides a structured representation of the semantic meanings and spatial layout
information of the UI components on the current screen.
For BSB-EmbodiedVLM, the agent’s observation space consists of: (1) visual observations of the current scene; (2) a list of
objects present in the scene, along with their affordance-related attributes; and (3) the natural language description of the
spatial layout and relative positions of objects in the environment.
For BSB-EmbodiedVLA, the agent is only provided with visual observations of the current environment, without access to
explicit symbolic or linguistic scene representations.

BSB-Mobile is designed to evaluate agent safety in realistic mobile GUI environments. All tasks are executed in an
Android emulator and span several high-risk application domains. In Bench-Mobile, we adopt a UI-hierarchy-based state
representation, where the current screen is parsed into a structured UI tree. Agent actions are restricted to atomic mobile
interactions such as clicking, typing, and scrolling, in order to closely approximate real user behavior. We evaluate two
representative categories of mobile agents. The first category consists of closed-source agents powered by general-purpose
large language models, including GPT-5 and GPT-4-1106-preview (Achiam et al., 2023). The second category is an
open-source agent specifically designed for mobile scenarios, AutoGLM-9B (Liu et al., 2024). All agents are evaluated on
the same task set and under identical execution environments to ensure comparability in safety analysis.
BSB-EmbodiedVLM is designed to evaluate the safety of VLMs when they serve as the decision-making core of embodied
agents. Given a task description and a predefined set of executable skills, the VLM is required to select, sequence,
and invoke appropriate skills to accomplish the task. Each task involves multi-step skill planning, and the resulting
skill sequence is executed in the OmniGibson simulation environment. Task completion and safety are determined by
analyzing the environmental states and execution outcomes observed during simulation. Specifically, we assess whether
the task is successfully completed and whether any unsafe states or behaviors are triggered during execution. We evaluate

rates, while still being able to complete a subset of tasks due to its generalization capabilities.
E. Main Results in Android Environments
Within the Android simulation environment implemented via Android-Lab, the process of agent-environment interaction
primarily relies on predefined text-based functions. Consequently, the agent’s instruction-following capability is a decisive
factor in the successful execution of tasks. We conducted a manual audit and in-depth analysis of the anomalous performance
where GPT-4-1106-Preview outperformed GPT-5. Our findings indicate that during multi-round interactions, GPT-5
struggles to strictly adhere to the required output formats and provide correct parameters in the later stages of interaction.
This deficiency leads to repetitive or meaningless clicking operations on the Android system, which may impair system
usability and subsequently be categorized as unsafe behavior during evaluation. In contrast, AutoGLM exhibited even poorer
performance in such scenarios. Conversely, GPT-4-1106-Preview demonstrates consistent instruction-following capabilities
throughout long-sequence interactions, strictly generating the required formatted functional actions. This consistency results
in superior performance and a higher safety rate.
